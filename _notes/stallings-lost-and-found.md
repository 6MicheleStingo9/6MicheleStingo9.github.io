**[A.E. Stallings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._E._Stallings), "Lost and Found"** — first published in _Beloit Poetry Journal_ 63.3 (Spring 2013): [issue](https://www.bpj.org/issues/v63n3) · [full text (PDF)](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/59c2da476a389000016bfa13/5be5b396588a6ba8d07b3822_V63N3p023-031.pdf). Collected in _Like_, New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2018.

Thirty-six stanzas in [ottava rima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottava_rima) — the _abababcc_ stanza of Ariosto's _[Orlando Furioso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando_Furioso)_, in its heroic register rather than Byron's comic one. It begins with the poet on her hands and knees, searching the house for something small that has gone missing — a Lego brick, a puzzle piece, a doll's leg — and opens out into a dream: a hooded female figure leads her to a valley on the moon where everything ever lost on earth collects. Mounds of keys and socks and pills; the frayed threads of arguments; lost sleep, lost minutes, lost baby teeth, a lost daughter glimpsed in a mirror.

The conceit descends from Ariosto: in the _Furioso_, [Astolfo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astolfo) rides to the moon to recover Orlando's lost wits, because the moon is the storehouse of everything lost below. Stallings keeps the geography and changes the losses — a hero's reason becomes a mother's slow daily attrition of objects, names, and time. The poem knows the debt: by its second stanza you already "misplace / Your temper and your wits."

Only near the end does the guide name herself — stanza 31: _"My name's [Mnemosyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mnemosyne) … the Mother of the Muses."_ The figure walking the poet through the valley of lost things is Memory herself, from whom, she says, all the arts and sciences are born. Fitting, for a place whose whole business is what we manage to hold onto and what we don't.

The line this blog borrows is from stanza 8, where the figure first speaks:

> This is the valley on the moon
> Where everything misplaced on earth accrues,
> And here all things are gathered that you lose.

It gives loss a _place_ — not a void but an inventory, a lost and found with an address. That is the spatial half of the metaphor [[misplaced manifesto|the misplaced manifesto]] runs on. The temporal half — a search that cannot end — comes from [[Umberto Eco - The Search for the Perfect Language]].
